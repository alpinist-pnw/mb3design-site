## The Principle

Every hour in the model saves multiples on the shop floor. Every hour on the shop floor saves multiples at the job site.

Installation is 33%+ of total project cost. When it goes wrong, it does not go 10% wrong — it goes catastrophically wrong.

---

## The Argument in One Line (deck slide)

> The model is cheaper than the shop floor.
> The shop floor is cheaper than the job site.
> The job site is cheaper than doing it twice.

---

## What Front-Loading Actually Looks Like

Front-loading means investing in digital design to a depth that would look excessive by conventional estimates — and coming out ahead.

On the Bay Area Discovery Museum (BADM), the estimating team quoted 1,728 hours of CAD/drawing labor. The actual investment was 4,487 hours — 2.6x. These are CAD and drawing hours only, not total project budget.

The front-loading included: full 3D modeling of the exhibit against laser-scanned as-built building conditions, virtual prototyping of all building connections, resolution of every tree-to-ceiling-joist tie-in before fabrication, and integrated leveling feet designed to eliminate on-site shimming.

Installation went smoother than expected. Rework was estimated at 60–80% less than what Pacific Studio normally expects on ambitious installations.

Pacific Studio adopted the front-load approach on virtually all subsequent projects. The proof is behavioral.

---

## Why This Matters for Clients

The "obvious" efficient choice — faster, cheaper in CAD time — is often the expensive one when you account for material, shipping, installation, and site constraints. The only way to make that evaluation with confidence is inside the 3D model. But the decision to evaluate it there in the first place comes from knowing what happens when you push complexity to the job site.

---

## The Cautionary Version

At a natural history museum in the Midwest, a survey crew missed a wall protruding in a basement corridor — not in the gallery (the scope of work) but in the loading dock area where exhibits had to pass through. The install crew could not navigate large exhibits through the corridor. They had to remove a row of facade windows and crane exhibits in through the opening. Crane rental, cost overruns, schedule impact.

If that corridor had been in the 3D model, exhibits would have been designed to come apart in smaller pieces and reassemble seamlessly in the gallery.

Uncertainty at the site level is the norm, not the exception. The question is always whether you've accounted for it in the model or discovered it during install.
